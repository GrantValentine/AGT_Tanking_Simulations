Step 1

First, how does the NBA draft work?

The system
1,001 lottery combinations
At the end of every season, the 14 teams that missed the playoffs enter a weighted lottery. The NBA assigns each team a share of 1,001 combinations based on how bad their record was. Worse record, more combinations, better shot at the top pick.
The best of the worst
5 combinations. 0.5% odds.
The 14th-ranked team, the one with the best record among teams that missed the playoffs, gets just 5 of those 1,001 combinations. That's a half-percent shot at the number one pick. Barely anything. They're essentially playing for position, not the jackpot.
The flat zone
140 combinations each. Same odds for all three.
The three worst teams each receive 140 combinations, giving each a 14% shot at the top pick. The 2019 reform deliberately flattened the odds at the very bottom so no team could corner the market on misery. Theoretically, that should make tanking less attractive. In practice, 14% is still a lot to chase.
The full picture
More balls, better odds. Simple enough.
That's the whole system. The worse your record, the more lottery combinations you hold. And once teams figured that out, some of them started optimizing for it, deliberately. The question is: how much is a top pick actually worth?
The breakdown
The worst team sits at the top. The 14th sits at the bottom.
Balls rearranged by rank. Rank 1 (worst record) has the most combinations and the most balls — their stack is widest. Rank 14 barely registers. The gradient is the incentive. That's what teams are optimizing for.

Step 3

The crossover point

Making the playoffs does more than just advance a team in the standings. It means two to four extra home games with sold-out arenas, national TV appearances that drive sponsorship deals, and a merchandise surge that can last for years. Economists estimate the total franchise value boost at around $200 million per playoff appearance. In our model, that's worth 200 points.

Now compare that to the lottery. The average lottery pick is worth about 24 points in player value. Even the number one pick, at 100 points, is only worth that if you win the lottery outright. So why would anyone tank? Because at some point, your playoff odds get low enough that competing isn't worth it anymore. Drag the slider to find that crossover.

Step 5

Five attempts to fix tanking

The NBA and researchers have proposed several mechanisms to reduce tanking. Each takes a different approach. Tap to explore how each one works.

NBA Lottery (Current Rules, 2019)
The three teams with the worst records each get a 14% chance at the number one pick, a flat zone designed to stop one team from hoarding the best odds. Teams ranked 4th through 14th worst get declining odds, from 12.5% down to 0.5%. Only the top four picks are lottery-determined. Picks 5 through 14 go by record.
This still incentivizes tanking. A worse record always means more lottery balls.
The baseline. Better than before 2019, but tanking still pays off.
Bilevel Ranking
Draft positions are locked in at game 70 of the 82-game season. Whatever your record is when standings freeze determines your lottery slot. The final 12 games can only affect playoff seeding, not draft position. After the lock, losing has exactly zero lottery benefit.
Why it cuts tanking: once standings lock, there's nothing to gain from losing. The last third of the season becomes honest competition.
Eliminates late-season tanking. Some early-season incentive still remains.
COLA: Carry-Over Lottery Allocation
Every team that misses the playoffs earns exactly one ticket per season, regardless of their record. Tickets accumulate across years and are spent when a team wins a high pick. Draft order goes to whoever has the most tickets. Since this season's wins and losses don't affect your ticket count, there's no point in losing on purpose.
Why it nearly eliminates tanking: your record this year has zero effect on your lottery position. There is nothing to optimize.
Near-complete incentive removal. The strongest theoretical guarantee.
Weighted Loss Mechanism
Lottery position is based on a cumulative loss score where early-season losses count far more than late ones. The weight decays exponentially. A loss in game one is worth twice as much as a loss in game 20, and four times as much as a loss in game 40. By midseason, tanking buys almost no lottery benefit.
Why it reduces tanking: the payoff from losing shrinks continuously. There's no single cutoff to game the system around.
Continuous decay. No hard cutoff to time tanking around.
NBA 3-2-1 Proposal
The NBA is introducing this as a 3-year pilot, starting as early as next season. The incentive is deliberately inverted. The three worst teams (Tier A) each get 2 lottery balls, fewer than any other lottery team. Teams ranked 4th through 14th worst (Tier B) each get 3 balls. The absolute worst teams are guaranteed no worse than pick 12. All 14 picks are lottery-determined, not just the top four.
Why it reduces tanking: landing in the bottom three gives you fewer lottery balls than everyone else. Teams near the bottom compete to stay out of Tier A.
Inverts the incentive. Being the worst is now actively bad for lottery odds.

The Simulation

How much does each reform actually cut tanking?

We ran 50 simulated NBA seasons under each mechanism with strategic teams that calculate their odds every game. Scroll to reveal each mechanism.

Mechanism 1 of 5
NBA Lottery (Current)
6.9%
The current system is the baseline. Strategic teams tank 6.9% of game-day decisions — every time they have a chance to win, they calculate whether it's worth it. That's the incentive the lottery creates.
Mechanism 2 of 5
Bilevel: Lock at Game 70
3.0%
Cutting the season in half for lottery purposes nearly halves tanking. The remaining 3.0% happens before the lock point. Teams still tank early to secure a better lottery slot before standings freeze.
Mechanism 3 of 5
Weighted Loss
2.3%
The exponential decay squeezes tanking windows throughout the year. Strategic teams still find pockets to optimize, but the payoff gets smaller the longer they wait.
Mechanism 4 of 5
NBA 3-2-1
0.9%
When being in the bottom three gives you fewer lottery balls, teams near the absolute bottom compete harder to avoid that zone. Only 0.9% of decisions are tanking. Near the noise floor.
Mechanism 5 of 5
COLA
0.5%
When this season's record has zero effect on lottery odds, rational teams essentially stop tanking. The 0.5% comes from early-season decisions before teams have enough data to rule out a playoff run.
Beyond Tanking

Does less tanking mean fairer drafts?

Suppressing tanking only matters if the mechanisms also help the teams that genuinely need good picks. We track two things for every mechanism.

How well do standings reflect true team quality?
We compare each season's final standings against each team's true underlying skill. A score of zero means the best teams won the most, perfectly. A higher score means more disorder between talent and results. All five mechanisms land in a tight band, meaning the reform design barely affects competitive balance at these tanking rates.
Average draft pick for the 8 weakest teams
Lower is better: weak teams get earlier picks.
The weakest quarter
3-2-1 gives the weakest teams the best picks.
Looking at the 8 teams with the lowest true skill each season, 3-2-1 delivers the lowest average pick number. That's because all 14 picks go through the lottery under 3-2-1, giving even middling weak teams a real shot at any slot instead of being locked into picks 5 through 8 by record.
Now zoom in on the bottom 3
COLA is hardest on newly terrible teams.
COLA rewards history, not this year's record. A team that was great for years and just fell apart this season hasn't accumulated many tickets yet. They could be genuinely the worst team in the league but still sit at the back of the lottery queue, behind franchises that have been bad longer. COLA struggles to quickly adapt to new information about which teams need help the most.
The AI Experiment

What if AI ran every front office?

We replaced every team's front office with a Claude Haiku language model, the same AI that powers many commercial chatbots. Each AI received real standings, game counts, and the rules of the mechanism, then had to decide: compete or tank? No hints. No training on the right answer.

NBA Lottery
8.0% vs 6.9% for strategic teams
A real front office doesn't run expected-value calculations on every game. A GM reads the standings, weighs fan expectations, thinks about whether a season still feels salvageable. The AI does the same — it applies a qualitative "we're out of it" threshold and drops effort sharply. That's actually closer to how front offices behave than a strict probability calculation. It's also why the AI tanks slightly more than the math demands: loss aversion and narrative framing push GMs to give up earlier than is optimal.
Bilevel
6.2% vs 3.0% strategic
The AI grasps the deadline mechanic — tank now before Game 70, or lose the opportunity entirely. It responds to this kind of bounded window more aggressively than a pure optimizer would. Real GMs show the same behavior: Thaler calls it present bias — the value of an action feels larger when time is running out, even if the math doesn't change.
Weighted Loss
3.4% vs 2.3% strategic
The AI picks up the decay mechanic and pulls back on late-season tanking. But it still overshoots. There are qualitative factors the AI weighs that don't fit a formula: the appeal of having your name attached to a high pick, the fear of disappointing fans who expected a tank-for-the-top narrative, the irrational stubbornness of committing to a strategy.
COLA
0.0% matches strategic
When your record this year has zero effect on lottery position, there is nothing to rationalize. The AI reads the rules and doesn't tank — not because it calculated expected values, but because it understands the situation doesn't reward it. This matters: even behaviorally messy decision-makers stop a behavior when the incentive is fully removed.
NBA 3-2-1
~0% matches strategic
The AI correctly identifies that landing in the bottom three is actively bad for lottery odds and competes accordingly. The inversion is intuitive enough that qualitative reasoning handles it.

One important caveat: all of this assumes a normal draft year. A generational talent like Wembanyama could spike the value of the top pick enough to flip the math — and a human GM with a city desperate for a superstar might chase that story even when the numbers say not to.
Policy Recommendation

What should the NBA do?

No mechanism eliminates tanking entirely. Any lottery that links record to picks will always have some team somewhere with a reason to lose. The goal is to make tanking costly enough that it only makes sense in extreme situations.

The NBA is expected to adopt the 3-2-1 format as soon as next season. Based on our simulations, that's the right call. But the tier boundary creates a new incentive worth watching closely.
What we support

3-2-1 cuts tanking from 6.9% to 0.9% in our simulations. All 14 picks go through the lottery, so even mediocre non-playoff teams get a real shot at any slot. Teams near the bottom three threshold have genuine reason to compete instead of lose. And it requires no new infrastructure. Just a rebalancing of balls.

0.9% tanking rate
What we worry about

3-2-1 creates a strange incentive at the tier boundary. The 3rd-worst team has fewer lottery balls than the 4th-worst. A franchise hovering at that line might actually try to win games to climb out of Tier A into better-odds Tier B. It's a new flavor of gaming the system, just inverted. And in a generational draft year — another Wembanyama — even the reduced Tier A odds might be worth chasing, because the top pick is worth so much more.

One more thing: the NBA is treating 3-2-1 as a 3-year experiment. If it doesn't reduce tanking in practice, they'll revisit.

Tier A 2 balls vs Tier B 3 balls: the inversion
Looking further ahead: if 3-2-1 proves effective, the NBA should consider building on it. Two options stand out. Layering in a Bilevel standings lock at game 70 would cut tanking further with no new infrastructure. Or the league could move toward a COLA-style format where lottery position is based on years out of the playoffs rather than current record, the approach with the strongest theoretical guarantee that tanking is never the smart move.
"Teams are smarter, they are creative, and they respond — we move, they move — so we're always looking to see whether there's yet a better system."
— Adam Silver, NBA Commissioner
